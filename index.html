Working Papers

Wind Turbine Entry and Transmission Expansion: Evaluating Spatial Subsidies in Germany (Job Market Paper)

Abstract

Ambitious renewable capacity targets place a lot of energy generation in resource-abundant areas far away from demand centers, putting strain on slowly-adapting transmission networks. I develop and estimate a spatial model of onshore wind-turbine entry and combine it with a network-constrained dispatch model of the German electricity system with endogenous congestion management. I compare the current wind-productivity-adjusted subsidy with spatially flat and congestion-adjusted support, before and after a planned grid expansion. Flat support raises generation costs by about two percent on the existing grid but performs similarly to the status quo after expansion, showing that locational correction is important during transition to a new grid. Congestion-adjusted support lowers generation costs by about two percent significantly lowering curtailment and, without grid expansion, achieves 75 percent of the cost reduction obtained by combining the status quo with the planned grid. Subsidy design and transmission expansion are therefore joint policy margins: congestion-adjusted support can partly substitute for grid investment, alleviating costs and improving renewable integration in transition while subsequently reducing the need for costly grid expansion.

Equilibrium Effects in Complementary Markets: Electric Vehicle Adoption and Electricity Pricing

With Kevin Remmy and Mathias Reynaert

Revise and resubmit at American Economic Review

Abstract

Electric vehicles shift passenger transport from oil to electricity, linking vehicle adoption to hourly power-market conditions. We develop and estimate a joint equilibrium model of German vehicle demand and electricity supply in which driver-specific charging decisions map travel profiles and electricity prices into EV operating costs and load. A 10% EV stock raises wholesale prices by 3.3%, creating sizable cost spillovers on non-EV electricity users, but reduces EV adoption by less than 1%. Time-varying tariffs lower charging costs and shift load to cheaper hours; in equilibrium, EV adoption offsets much of the system-cost relief while redirecting generator profits toward renewables.
